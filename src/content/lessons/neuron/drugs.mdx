## Every step is a target

In <a href={siteUrl('/chapters/neuron/synapse/')}>lesson 2.5</a> you followed one spike across a synapse. Each step in that chain is a place where a drug can act:

1. **Making the transmitter.** Give more of the raw material and the terminal can make more. L-DOPA, a treatment for Parkinson's disease, works this way.
2. **Filling the vesicles.** Reserpine stops vesicles filling with monoamines, so there is little left to release.
3. **Release.** Botulinum toxin stops vesicles fusing. Black widow venom forces them to fuse all at once.
4. **Receptors.** A drug can sit on the receptor and switch it on, like nicotine, or sit there and block it, like curare.
5. **Reuptake.** Blocking the transporters keeps transmitter in the cleft for longer. Cocaine and antidepressants such as fluoxetine (Prozac) do this.
6. **Breakdown.** Blocking the enzyme that destroys a transmitter makes it last longer. Nerve agents block acetylcholinesterase.
7. **Autoreceptors.** Blocking the terminal's own brake can increase release.

## Agonists and antagonists

Two words cover most drugs:

- An <Term id="agonist">agonist</Term> boosts the effect of a transmitter.
- An <Term id="antagonist">antagonist</Term> weakens or blocks it.

In this course we use the words broadly: a drug that blocks reuptake is an agonist, because it makes the transmitter work harder, even though it never touches the receptor. Pharmacologists often use the words more narrowly, for drugs that bind the receptor itself. In that narrow sense, an agonist binds and activates the receptor, and a *competitive* antagonist binds the same spot without activating it, so less transmitter can get on.

<Notice>

1. **Each drug acts at one step.** The warning ring shows where: the receptors, the transporters, the enzyme, or the vesicles.
2. **Receptor blockers leave release alone.** With curare, the terminal releases as much acetylcholine as before. The postsynaptic response shrinks because fewer receptors can be reached.
3. **Clearance drugs stretch the message.** Blocking reuptake or the enzyme keeps transmitter in the cleft, so the PSP grows and lasts longer.
4. **A drug with no target does nothing.** Acetylcholine is not taken back up, so a reuptake blocker has no effect at that synapse.
5. **Some drugs act without any spike.** An agonist or an MDMA-like drug changes the postsynaptic voltage before you send a single spike. Look at the starting voltage.

</Notice>

## One transmitter, opposite effects

Here is the key idea of this lesson. A transmitter is not excitatory or inhibitory by itself. **The receptor decides.**

Remember Loewi's frog heart from lesson 2.5. Acetylcholine *slowed* the heart. Yet at the synapse between a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle, acetylcholine makes the muscle *contract*. Same molecule, opposite effects. The muscle has nicotinic receptors, which are channels that let Na⁺ in. The heart has muscarinic receptors, which work through a G protein to open K⁺ channels and slow the beat.

So drugs can be very selective. Atropine blocks muscarinic receptors but not nicotinic ones: it speeds up the heart and widens the pupils, but leaves your muscles working. Curare does the reverse.

<UnderTheHood title="Under the hood: how the drug bench works">

The drug bench changes one number in the synapse model from lesson 2.5, and a second copy of the model runs alongside without the drug, to draw the dashed lines.

- **Agonist.** Adds a steady amount of receptor activation, as if a drug were always bound to some receptors. That is why the postsynaptic voltage moves before any spike.
- **Receptor blocker.** Competes with the transmitter for the receptors. Lots of transmitter can still outcompete it, which is what *competitive* means.
- **Reuptake blocker.** Cuts the transporters' clearance rate to a tenth.
- **Enzyme blocker.** Cuts the enzyme's clearance rate to a twentieth. At a serotonin or GABA synapse, the enzyme sits inside the terminal instead, so blocking it lets each vesicle hold more transmitter.
- **Release blocker.** Cuts the chance that a docked vesicle fuses to a twenty-fifth. For serotonin, the example is reserpine, so the vesicles still fuse but are nearly empty.
- **Release booster.** Multiplies the chance of fusion by about two and a half. For serotonin, the example is MDMA, so transmitter leaks out through the transporters, even without spikes.

If a synapse has nothing for the drug to act on, the bench says so and the model is unchanged.

</UnderTheHood>

<FinePrint>

- **Real drugs are messier.** Most act at more than one site. Cocaine blocks the transporters for dopamine, noradrenaline and serotonin. Alcohol affects GABA, glutamate and several other receptors.
- **Some drugs change how well a receptor works.** Benzodiazepines such as diazepam bind to their own site on the GABA-A receptor. They do nothing alone, but make the receptor respond more strongly when GABA binds. They are agonists in the broad sense, not the narrow one.
- **The brain adapts.** Antidepressants block reuptake within hours, but their effect on mood usually takes weeks. With repeated use of many drugs, the brain changes its receptors and release to compensate. That is part of tolerance and withdrawal.
- **Doses matter.** The bench uses one strong dose of each drug. In reality, a little botulinum toxin relaxes one muscle and a lot can stop breathing.
- **The muscle junction is simplified.** In the simulation the acetylcholine synapse makes a small EPSP. At a real nerve and muscle junction, the response is much larger.

</FinePrint>

## Common questions

<Faq q="Why do antidepressants take weeks to work if they block reuptake in hours?">
Nobody knows the full answer yet. Blocking reuptake is only the first step. Over the following weeks, autoreceptors become less sensitive, receptor numbers change, and new connections may form. The slower changes seem to matter for mood.
</Faq>

<Faq q="Is caffeine a drug at the synapse?">
Yes. Caffeine blocks receptors for adenosine, a chemical that builds up while you are awake and makes you feel sleepy. Blocking a calming signal makes you feel more alert. So caffeine is an antagonist that has a stimulating effect.
</Faq>

<Faq q="If Parkinson's disease is a lack of dopamine, why not just give dopamine?">
Dopamine in the blood cannot cross the blood-brain barrier into the brain. Its building block, L-DOPA, can. Neurons then turn L-DOPA into dopamine themselves.
</Faq>

<Faq q="Can a drug be an agonist at one receptor and an antagonist at another?">
Yes, and many are. Some antipsychotics, for example, block certain dopamine receptors and several serotonin receptors at the same time. That is one reason drugs have side effects.
</Faq>
