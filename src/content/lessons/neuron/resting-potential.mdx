<Hook title="A small number that can stop a heart">
Healthy blood contains about 4 millimoles of potassium per litre. When the kidneys fail, that number can creep up to 7 or 8. It sounds like a tiny change, and the patient may feel nothing more than a little weak. Yet doctors treat it as an emergency, because it can make the heart stop beating. Why should a few extra potassium ions outside the cells matter so much? The answer is the resting potential, and by the end of this lesson you will be able to predict the danger yourself.
</Hook>

## A battery in every cell

Put one fine electrode inside a neuron and another in the fluid outside, and you measure a voltage: the <Term id="membrane-potential">membrane potential</Term>. In a neuron that is not being stimulated, the inside is about 70 millivolts more negative than the outside. That is the <Term id="resting-potential">resting potential</Term>, written as −70 mV.

The voltage comes from <Term id="ion">ions</Term>, atoms with an electric charge, which are spread unevenly across the membrane:

<div class="table-scroll">

| Ion | Inside the cell | Outside the cell | Where it is crowded |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Potassium, K⁺ | about 140 mM | about 5 mM | inside |
| Sodium, Na⁺ | about 12 mM | about 145 mM | outside |
| Chloride, Cl⁻ | about 10 mM | about 110 mM | outside |
| Large proteins, A⁻ | many | almost none | inside, and too big to leave |

</div>

(mM means millimoles per litre, a measure of how crowded an ion is.)

Two forces act on every ion:

- **<Term id="diffusion">Diffusion</Term>.** Ions jiggle about at random, so they spread from where they are crowded to where they are scarce. A <Term id="concentration-gradient">concentration gradient</Term> pushes them across.
- **<Term id="electrostatic-pressure">Electrostatic pressure</Term>.** Opposite charges attract and like charges repel. A negative inside pulls positive ions in and pushes negative ions out.

The resting potential is what happens when these two forces meet.

<Notice>

1. **Each K⁺ that leaves makes the inside a little more negative.** Positive charge leaves, and a negative protein (A⁻) is left behind. The charge builds up as a thin layer on each face of the membrane.
2. **The pull grows, the push does not.** As the inside becomes negative, the *pull* arrow grows. The coloured *push* arrow stays the same, because the concentrations hardly change.
3. **The voltage levels off at −89 mV.** That is where pull equals push: potassium's <Term id="equilibrium-potential">equilibrium potential</Term>, E<sub>K</sub>, the dashed line on the plot.
4. **Ions still cross at equilibrium.** Look at the bars under the plot: K⁺ keeps crossing both ways, but equally often. The balance is dynamic, not frozen.
5. **Very few ions are needed.** The readout under the plot says how many ions a real cell needs: millions, but about 0.001 percent of the K⁺ inside.

</Notice>

## The Nernst equation

Each ion has its own equilibrium potential, set only by its concentrations on the two sides and its charge. The <Term id="nernst-equation">Nernst equation</Term> gives it. At body temperature, for an ion with one positive charge, it simplifies to:

<Math display tex="E_{\text{ion}} = 61.5\ \text{mV} \times \log_{10}\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{out}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{in}}}" />

For potassium, 61.5 × log<sub>10</sub>(5 / 140) gives −89 mV. Every tenfold difference in concentration is worth about 61.5 millivolts. For sodium you get about +67 mV, because sodium is crowded outside. Chloride is crowded outside too, but it is negative, so its equilibrium potential is negative: about −64 mV.

## Real membranes leak more than one ion

A real neuron at rest is not −89 mV. It is closer to −70 mV. That is because its membrane has <Term id="leak-channel">leak channels</Term> for several ions at once. Each ion pulls the voltage towards its own equilibrium potential, like teams in a tug-of-war. How hard each team pulls depends on how easily its ion can cross: its <Term id="permeability">permeability</Term>.

At rest, the membrane is most permeable to K⁺, a lot less to Cl⁻, and only slightly to Na⁺. The <Term id="goldman-equation">Goldman equation</Term> combines them.

## What the pump really does

The <Term id="sodium-potassium-pump">sodium-potassium pump</Term> is a protein that uses energy from ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out of the cell and 2 K⁺ in. It puts back exactly what the leak channels let through. The pump and the leaks keep running all the time, which is why your brain uses so much energy even when you are resting.

Because the pump moves three positive charges out for every two it brings in, it makes the inside slightly more negative by itself, by a few millivolts. But its main job is to keep the concentration gradients. If it stops, the voltage does not collapse at once. The leaks slowly wear the gradients down: K⁺ leaks out, Na⁺ leaks in, and over hours the resting potential runs down towards 0 mV.

## Back to the patient

Now you can explain the emergency. Extra potassium outside the cell makes the concentration gradient for K⁺ smaller. A smaller gradient means a weaker push, so E<sub>K</sub> moves up, and the resting potential moves up with it. In experiment 3, raising K⁺ outside from 5 to 12 mM moves the resting potential from about −72 to −59 mV.

A cell that rests closer to threshold might sound easier to fire. But a membrane that stays depolarised keeps its sodium channels inactivated (lesson 2.3), so heart muscle cells stop firing properly. The heart's rhythm breaks down. That is why potassium levels in the blood are watched so closely.

<UnderTheHood title="Under the hood: Nernst, Goldman and the two simulations">

**The full Nernst equation.** For an ion with charge *z*:

<Math display label="Nernst equation" tex="E_{\text{ion}} = \frac{RT}{zF}\,\ln\frac{[\text{ion}]_{\text{out}}}{[\text{ion}]_{\text{in}}}" />

Here *R* is the gas constant, *T* the temperature in kelvin and *F* Faraday's constant. At 37 °C, *RT/F* is 26.7 mV. Multiply by ln(10) ≈ 2.303 to get the 61.5 mV per tenfold change used above. The equation comes from setting the energy an ion gains by moving down its concentration gradient equal to the electrical energy it must spend to cross the voltage.

**The Goldman equation.** For K⁺, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ with permeabilities *P*:

<Math display label="Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation" tex="V_{\text{rest}} = \frac{RT}{F}\,\ln\frac{P_{\text{K}}[\text{K}^+]_{\text{out}} + P_{\text{Na}}[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{out}} + P_{\text{Cl}}[\text{Cl}^-]_{\text{in}}}{P_{\text{K}}[\text{K}^+]_{\text{in}} + P_{\text{Na}}[\text{Na}^+]_{\text{in}} + P_{\text{Cl}}[\text{Cl}^-]_{\text{out}}}" />

Chloride's inside and outside are swapped because its charge is negative. With *P*<sub>K</sub> : *P*<sub>Na</sub> : *P*<sub>Cl</sub> = 1 : 0.04 : 0.45 and the concentrations in the table, the numerator is 15.3 and the denominator 190.0, so V<sub>rest</sub> = 26.7 × ln(15.3 / 190.0) = −67.3 mV. With only one ion permeable, the equation becomes that ion's Nernst equation.

**The pump.** With the pump running, the builder uses the Mullins–Noda form, in which the potassium terms are multiplied by the pump's ratio, 3/2. That adds about −4 mV, which is why the typical neuron shows −71.7 mV.

**How the tug-of-war works.** Each drawn ion that crosses changes the voltage by 3 mV. Ions cross at random, with a rate in each direction proportional to the concentration they start from, times a factor e<sup>±zFV/2RT</sup> for the voltage. When the two rates are equal, the voltage is exactly the Nernst potential, so the simulation settles there and keeps flickering around it.

**How thick is each rope?** In the builder, each ion's share of the pull is its *chord conductance*: the current it would carry divided by how far the voltage is from its equilibrium potential. The resting potential is then exactly the share-weighted average of the three equilibrium potentials.

</UnderTheHood>

<FinePrint>

- **Concentrations vary.** The values here are typical for a mammalian neuron. Different cells and textbooks use slightly different numbers, so resting potentials between −60 and −80 mV are all normal.
- **Chloride is often near balance.** In many neurons chloride simply settles where the other ions put the voltage, so E<sub>Cl</sub> is close to the resting potential. Some neurons pump chloride to set it differently.
- **The bulk stays neutral.** The tug-of-war draws the extra charge as a thin layer on each face of the membrane. Everywhere else, inside and outside, positive and negative charges still balance.
- **The run-down is simplified.** The builder keeps the fluid outside fixed and lets only the inside change. A real cell without a pump also swells, as water follows the ions in. How fast the gradients run down depends on the cell's size.
- **The Goldman equation is an approximation.** It assumes that ions cross independently of each other. It works well for the resting potential.

</FinePrint>

## Common questions

<Faq q="Doesn't the sodium-potassium pump create the resting potential?">
Only indirectly. The voltage is made by ions leaking through channels, mostly K⁺. The pump's job is to keep the concentration gradients that those leaks need. Its own direct effect is a few millivolts. Stop the pump, and the resting potential only fades slowly as the gradients run down.
</Faq>

<Faq q="If K⁺ keeps leaking out, won't the cell run out of potassium?">
No. Only a tiny fraction of the K⁺ has to leave to set up the voltage, and once the voltage is there, the leak in each direction balances. The small steady leak that remains, together with the sodium leak, is undone by the pump.
</Faq>

<Faq q="The cell is full of positive K⁺. How can the inside be negative?">
Because the K⁺ inside is balanced by negative ions, mostly large proteins. The voltage comes from a tiny imbalance in a thin layer right at the membrane: a few positive ions too many on the outside face, and a few negative ones too many on the inside face. The rest of the cell stays neutral.
</Faq>

<Faq q="Is the resting potential always −70 mV?">
No. It is usually between about −60 and −80 mV, and it differs between types of neuron. Glial cells, which leak almost only potassium, often rest closer to −85 mV. And every time the membrane's permeability changes, as it does during an action potential, the voltage changes with it.
</Faq>
