## One neuron, thousands of voices

In <a href={siteUrl('/chapters/neuron/synapse/')}>lesson 2.5</a>, one spike at one synapse made a postsynaptic potential of a few millivolts. A neuron in your cortex receives thousands of synapses, some excitatory and some inhibitory. To fire, its voltage has to climb from rest to threshold, about 15 millivolts in this lesson's model. No single <Term id="epsp">EPSP</Term> gets there.

The decision is made at the <Term id="axon-hillock">axon hillock</Term>, where the axon leaves the cell body. It has many voltage-gated sodium channels, so it has the lowest threshold in the cell. Whatever voltage reaches the hillock decides whether an action potential starts, just as in <a href={siteUrl('/chapters/neuron/action-potential/')}>lesson 2.3</a>.

Postsynaptic potentials are not like action potentials. They are *graded*: their size depends on how much transmitter arrived. And they are *passive*: nothing rebuilds them as they spread. So they shrink in two ways:

- **With distance.** A PSP leaks away as it spreads along a dendrite towards the cell body. The <Term id="length-constant">length constant</Term>, λ, is the distance over which it shrinks to about a third of its size.
- **With time.** A PSP fades after it peaks. The <Term id="time-constant">time constant</Term>, τ, sets how fast.

<Notice>

1. **One EPSP is not enough.** Even a synapse right on the cell body lifts the hillock by about 12 millivolts, short of the 15 needed.
2. **Inputs at the same time add up.** This is <Term id="spatial-summation">spatial summation</Term>: inputs from different places, summed at the hillock.
3. **Inputs close together in time add up.** This is <Term id="temporal-summation">temporal summation</Term>: a second EPSP that arrives before the first has faded starts from higher up.
4. **Distance matters.** A synapse 400 micrometres out on a dendrite makes an EPSP at the hillock that is several times smaller, later and more spread out than one on the cell body.
5. **Inhibition near the cell body is powerful.** One IPSP on the cell body can cancel two EPSPs that would otherwise fire the cell. The same inhibitory synapse far out on a dendrite hardly matters.

</Notice>

## Spatial summation

Each synapse injects a little current. At the hillock, the voltage changes add: two EPSPs of 8 millivolts make roughly 16. An excitatory and an inhibitory input partly cancel. A neuron fires when the sum of everything arriving at the hillock reaches threshold. With thousands of inputs, the neuron is constantly adding and subtracting.

## Temporal summation

An EPSP does not vanish at once. It decays over tens of milliseconds, at a speed set by the membrane's time constant. If the same synapse fires again before the first EPSP has gone, the second one starts from a higher level. Fire fast enough and the EPSPs pile up past threshold.

This is how a single, very active input can drive a neuron on its own, and how timing carries information. A neuron with a long time constant adds up inputs over a wide window. One with a short time constant only responds to inputs that arrive almost together.

## Where a synapse sits matters

A PSP that starts far out on a thin dendrite leaks out through the membrane on its way to the cell body. The further it travels, the smaller it gets. A long length constant means less is lost.

Inhibition follows the same rule, even more strongly. An inhibitory synapse opens chloride channels. Near the cell body, those open channels hold the hillock close to rest and short-circuit the excitatory current flowing in from the dendrites. Far out on a dendrite, their effect stays mostly local. This is why many inhibitory neurons in the cortex make their synapses on the cell body, or even on the start of the axon: that is where a "stop" is hardest to ignore.

## A neuron makes decisions

Put these ideas together and a neuron starts to look like a tiny decision-maker. Each input has a *weight*: how much it moves the hillock, which depends on the synapse's strength, its sign (excitatory or inhibitory) and its distance. The neuron adds up the weighted inputs and fires if the total passes a threshold.

Try the AND gate below. Neither input can fire the cell alone, but both together can. Make each input strong enough to fire the cell on its own and you would have an OR gate instead. Chapter 3 builds whole networks from exactly this idea.

<UnderTheHood title="Under the hood: a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron">

The board uses one of the oldest and most useful models in neuroscience: a *leaky integrate-and-fire* neuron. The voltage at the hillock, *V*, leaks back to rest with time constant τ<sub>m</sub>, is pushed up by excitatory current, and is pulled towards chloride's equilibrium potential by inhibitory conductance:

<Math display label="Hillock voltage" tex="\frac{dV}{dt} = -\frac{V - V_{\text{rest}}}{\tau_m} + I_{\text{exc}}(t) - g_{\text{inh}}(t)\,(V - E_{\text{Cl}})" />

When *V* reaches −55 millivolts, the model records a spike, resets the voltage to −75 millivolts and holds it there for 3 milliseconds. Rest is −70 millivolts and E<sub>Cl</sub> is −80.

Each input reaches the hillock as a smooth bump in current or conductance. Its size shrinks with distance *x* from the cell body, as it would along a passive cable:

<Math display label="Attenuation with distance" tex="w = e^{-x/\lambda}" />

Distant inputs also arrive a little later and more spread out in time. Inhibition uses a length constant half as long, because a conductance far away changes the hillock voltage much less than one close by. Real dendrites need many coupled equations to describe properly; this shortcut keeps the parts you can see on the board.

</UnderTheHood>

<FinePrint>

- **Real dendrites are not passive.** Many have voltage-gated channels that boost distant inputs or even fire small dendritic spikes of their own. So a distant synapse can matter more than this model suggests.
- **The numbers are chosen to be easy to see.** A real EPSP from one synapse is often well under a millivolt by the time it reaches the cell body, and a neuron may need dozens of them at once. Here, two or three are enough.
- **The spike is drawn, not modelled.** The model does not have sodium and potassium channels. It simply records a spike at threshold and resets.
- **Threshold is not fixed.** In real neurons it varies with recent activity, as lesson 2.3 showed.

</FinePrint>

## Common questions

<Faq q="Does the axon hillock really add things up, like a calculator?">
Not by counting. The currents from all the open synapses flow through the cell and change the voltage together, and voltage changes simply combine. The result at the hillock is roughly the sum, which is why "adding up" is a good description.
</Faq>

<Faq q="If one EPSP is so small, why have so many synapses?">
Because the neuron is meant to respond to patterns, not to single events. A cell that fires only when many of its inputs agree is a reliable detector. A cell that fired on any one input would be easily fooled by noise.
</Faq>

<Faq q="Is a neuron really a logic gate?">
It can act like one, as the AND gate experiment shows. But a real neuron is richer: its inputs have different weights, its dendrites do some processing of their own, and timing matters. Chapter 3 starts from the simple version and shows how far it can go.
</Faq>

<Faq q="What sets a neuron's time constant?">
The membrane's resistance and capacitance. A membrane with few open channels holds charge for longer and has a longer time constant. Open many channels, for example during strong inhibition, and the time constant gets shorter.
</Faq>
