<Hook title="Brain soup">
For decades, textbooks said that glial cells outnumber neurons ten to one. Nobody had actually counted. In 2009, a team in Brazil led by Suzana Herculano-Houzel dissolved whole human brains into a soup of free-floating cell nuclei, stained them, and counted. They found about 86 billion neurons and about 85 billion other cells, most of them glia. Roughly one to one. In this lesson you will meet both kinds of cell, and see why each one matters.
</Hook>

## Two kinds of cell

Your nervous system is built from two kinds of cell.

- <Term id="neuron">Neurons</Term> carry signals. They receive messages from other cells, combine them, and decide whether to send a message of their own.
- <Term id="glia">Glia</Term> look after the neurons. They insulate axons, feed and clean the space around neurons, and defend the brain against damage and infection.

Both live in two big regions. The <Term id="central-nervous-system">central nervous system</Term> (CNS) is the brain and the spinal cord. The <Term id="peripheral-nervous-system">peripheral nervous system</Term> (PNS) is all the nerves that link the CNS to the rest of the body. A nerve is a bundle of many axons, wrapped together like the wires in a cable.

The simulation below shows one neuron that crosses both regions: a motor neuron. Its cell body sits in your spinal cord, and its axon runs out through a nerve to a muscle. Around it are the four main kinds of glia.

## The neuron, part by part

Most neurons share the same basic plan. Think of it as a path that information follows.

1. **Dendrites** are the input branches. Other neurons make contact with them at synapses, often on tiny knobs called <Term id="dendritic-spine">dendritic spines</Term>. One neuron can receive thousands of synapses.
2. **The cell body**, or soma, holds the nucleus with the cell's DNA. It builds the proteins the whole neuron needs, right out to the tips of its axon. Small voltage changes from the dendrites meet here and add up.
3. **The axon hillock** is the cone where the axon leaves the cell body. It is packed with voltage-gated sodium channels, which makes it the easiest place to start an action potential (lesson 2.3).
4. **The axon** carries the action potential away from the cell body. Most neurons have one axon, although it can branch.
5. **Myelin** is a fatty wrapping made by glia. It speeds the signal up a lot (lesson 2.4).
6. **Terminal buttons** sit at the ends of the axon's branches. When an action potential arrives, they release a chemical messenger, a neurotransmitter, onto the next cell. That junction is a synapse (lesson 2.5).

## Three shapes, three jobs

Neurons come in several shapes. One way to sort them is by how many branches leave the cell body. Open the **Neuron shapes** tab in the simulation to see all three.

<div class="table-scroll">

| Shape | What it looks like | Where you find it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Term id="multipolar-neuron">Multipolar</Term> | One axon, many dendrites | Motor neurons, and most neurons in the brain |
| <Term id="bipolar-neuron">Bipolar</Term> | One dendrite and one axon, on opposite sides | Smell neurons in the nose; relay cells in the retina |
| <Term id="unipolar-neuron">Unipolar</Term> (or pseudo-unipolar) | One stalk that splits in two | Touch and pain neurons, with cell bodies just outside the spinal cord |

</div>

Another way to sort neurons is by their job. <Term id="sensory-neuron">Sensory neurons</Term> carry information from the senses into the CNS. <Term id="motor-neuron">Motor neurons</Term> carry commands out to muscles and glands. <Term id="interneuron">Interneurons</Term> connect neurons to each other inside the CNS, and they are by far the most common kind. Shape and job go together: a touch neuron's split stalk lets the signal run straight from your skin to your spinal cord without a detour through the cell body.

## Glia: much more than glue

The word glia comes from the Greek for glue. Nineteenth-century scientists thought these cells simply held the neurons together. We now know they do a lot more.

<div class="table-scroll">

| Glial cell | Where | Main jobs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Astrocyte | Brain and spinal cord | Links neurons to blood vessels; clears spare potassium and neurotransmitter; keeps the fluid around neurons right; wraps synapses; holds neurons in place |
| Oligodendrocyte | Brain and spinal cord | Makes myelin; one cell wraps segments of many axons |
| Schwann cell | Nerves of the body | Makes myelin; one cell wraps one segment of one axon; guides axons that regrow after injury |
| <Term id="microglia">Microglia</Term> | Brain and spinal cord | Immune defence; swallow debris and microbes; start inflammation after damage; prune unused synapses |

</div>

The Schwann cell's role in repair has a big practical consequence. If you cut a nerve in your finger, the Schwann cells clear away the broken part of the axon and line up to form a tube. The axon can regrow along that tube, slowly, about a millimetre a day. In the brain and spinal cord, oligodendrocytes do not do this, and the scar left by other glia blocks regrowth. That is one reason why spinal cord injuries are so hard to heal.

<UnderTheHood title="Under the hood: how do you count 86 billion cells?">

You cannot count cells one by one in a slice of brain and multiply up, because the density of cells varies hugely between brain regions. Herculano-Houzel's team used a method called the *isotropic fractionator*. They turned each part of the brain into a liquid in which every cell nucleus floats free, so the nuclei are spread evenly. Then they only had to count the nuclei in a few small drops and scale up by volume:

<Math display label="Scaling up from a sample" tex="N_{\text{total}} = \frac{n_{\text{counted}}}{V_{\text{sample}}} \times V_{\text{suspension}}" />

To tell neurons from other cells, they stained the nuclei with an antibody against NeuN, a protein found only in neuron nuclei. The rest were counted as non-neuronal cells.

The result surprised many people. In the cerebral cortex there are about three to four other cells for every neuron. In the cerebellum, which is packed with tiny neurons, there are far more neurons than glia. Across the whole brain, it comes out at roughly one to one.

</UnderTheHood>

<FinePrint>

- **The drawing is not to scale.** A real motor axon is thousands of times longer than the cell body is wide. Real myelin segments are about 100 times as long as the axon is thick, and a real oligodendrocyte can wrap dozens of axons, not three.
- **Spinal motor neurons have few spines.** We drew spines on this one because spines are where most excitatory synapses land on neurons in the brain, such as the pyramidal cells of the cortex.
- **One motor neuron, thousands of synapses.** The drawing shows a single incoming synapse. A real motor neuron receives thousands, and many of them are inhibitory (lesson 2.6).
- **The non-neuronal count is not only glia.** About 85 billion non-neuronal cells includes the cells of the blood vessels as well as glia, so the ratio of glia to neurons is close to, or a little under, one to one.
- **Other glia exist.** For example, ependymal cells line the fluid-filled ventricles of the brain, and the satellite cells around sensory cell bodies are glia of the PNS.

</FinePrint>

## Common questions

<Faq q="Is it true that there are ten times more glia than neurons?">
No. It was repeated in textbooks for decades, but careful counting finds roughly one glial cell per neuron across the whole brain. The ratio does vary between regions: the cortex has more glia than neurons, the cerebellum far fewer.
</Faq>

<Faq q="Are glia just support cells that don't do anything interesting?">
Far from it. Without myelin from glia, signals would be too slow for you to walk. Astrocytes control the fluid around neurons and help clear neurotransmitter, so they shape how synapses work. Microglia prune synapses during development. Many brain diseases, from multiple sclerosis to some forms of dementia, involve glia.
</Faq>

<Faq q="Is myelin part of the neuron?">
No. Myelin is made by glia: each myelin segment is a flattened arm of an oligodendrocyte or a whole Schwann cell, wrapped many times round the axon. That is why diseases that attack glia can stop neurons from working, even when the neurons themselves are healthy at first.
</Faq>

<Faq q="Does every neuron have an axon and dendrites?">
Almost every neuron has one axon, which can branch many times at its end. The number of dendrites varies with the shape: many in a multipolar neuron, one in a bipolar neuron. In a unipolar touch neuron, the branch from the skin behaves like an axon even though it brings information in.
</Faq>

<Faq q="Do adults make new neurons?">
Very few. Most neurons you have now were made before birth, and they cannot divide. Adults may add some new neurons in a few places, such as the hippocampus, but how many is still debated. Glial cells, on the other hand, can divide throughout life.
</Faq>
